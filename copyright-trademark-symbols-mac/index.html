How to type ©, ™ and ® on a Mac

Three marks, three Option keys: G for copyright, 2 for trademark, R for registered. Below: the rarer ℠ and ℗, why (c) turns into © in Pages but not in Mail, what changes on a British keyboard, and a plain-English note on which mark you’re allowed to use.

By Wells Riley, who makes Mojito·Updated October 4, 2026·Tested on macOS 27

⌥option G → ©

⌥ 2 types ™ · ⌥ R types ®

How do I type the copyright symbol on a Mac?

Press Option + G to type ©. The other two are on the same layer: Option + 2 types ™, and Option + R types ®. All three work in every app, from Pages and Google Docs to a caption field in Safari, with no setup. Those are the US keys. On the British layout, Option + 2 types € instead, and ™ moves to Shift + Option + 2.

With Mojito, type ::copy and press Return for ©, ::tm for ™, and ::reg for ®. The marks no keyboard has are in there too, like ::sm for ℠.

5 ways to type ©, ™ and ® on a Mac

  1. The Option keys: ⌥ G for ©, ⌥ 2 for ™, ⌥ R for ®. Fastest, and they work in every app.
  2. The Character Viewer: press Fn + E (or Control + Command + Space) to open the emoji picker, click the expand button in the panel’s top-right corner, search for copyright, trademark, or registered, and double-click the result. This is also where ℠ and ℗ live. The compact emoji picker has ©️, ™️, and ®️ as well, but as emoji, which some apps draw differently from the text around them.
  3. Auto-substitution in Pages, Numbers, and Keynote: type (c) and it becomes © as you type, as long as Symbol and text substitution is turned on in the app’s Auto-Correction settings. More on where this does and doesn’t work below.
  4. A text replacement: in System SettingsKeyboardText Replacements…, click + and map something like ;tm to ™. It syncs to your iPhone through iCloud, but it only fires in apps built on Apple’s text system, so not in Chrome, Slack, or VS Code.
  5. A shortcode: with Mojito installed, type ::copy, ::tm, or ::reg in any text field and press Return. ::sm gives you ℠, and ::sound finds ℗.

Why does (c) turn into © on its own?

Because the app you’re in does it, not macOS. Pages, Numbers, and Keynote swap (c) for © when Symbol and text substitution is on, under PagesSettingsAuto-Correction (Numbers and Keynote have the same setting). Apple’s own guide is explicit that those substitutions apply only to that app. Microsoft Word does the same thing through its own AutoCorrect list, under WordPreferencesAutoCorrect.

Everywhere else (Mail, Notes, TextEdit, and Safari included), (c) stays (c). The Mac’s system-wide text replacements ship with exactly one entry, omw for “On my way!”, so if you want (c) to become © in every app, add that replacement yourself. It’s a separate system from the smart quotes and dashes that turn -- into an em dash.

The usual victim is a lettered list: (a), (b), ©. To stop it, turn off Symbol and text substitution or delete just the (c) row from its list. In Word, delete the (c) entry from AutoCorrect.

™ or ®? What each mark means

  • ™ Trademark: a claim that a name, logo, or slogan identifies your goods. You can use it whether or not you’ve applied to register anything.
  • ℠ Service mark: the same claim, for services instead of goods.
  • ® Registered: only once the mark is actually registered. In the US, that means after the USPTO registers it (not while the application is pending), and only for the goods and services listed in the registration.
  • © Copyright: a copyright notice. In the US it has been optional for works published since March 1, 1989, and using it doesn’t require registering anything. The Copyright Office notes it still carries legal benefits. The format is the symbol (or the word “Copyright”), the year of first publication, and the owner: © 2026 Northwind Studio. “All rights reserved” isn’t one of the parts.
  • ℗ Sound recording copyright: the notice for sound recordings on records, tapes, CDs, and the like. It’s the © of the audio itself.

That’s how the US Copyright Office and the USPTO describe the symbols. It isn’t legal advice, and other countries have their own rules, so ask a trademark or IP lawyer about your situation.

Should ® be superscript?

™ and ℠ are drawn as small raised letters by design. © and ® usually sit at full size, which can look heavy next to a brand name, so they’re often set smaller and raised. Apple’s own Pages guide suggests superscript for exactly this: select the mark and press ⌃ ⇧ ⌘ + (Control-Shift-Command-Plus), or choose Superscript from the Baseline menu in the Format sidebar’s font options. On the web, wrap it in <sup>.

What about British and other keyboards?

Apple’s main European layouts all have the three marks, just not always on the same keys. These come from the keyboard layouts in macOS 27 (the US column also covers ABC, Australian, and Canadian English):

On a British Mac, ⌥ 2 is the euro sign; the rest of the money symbols are in Currency symbols on a Mac. Shortcodes skip the whole question, since ::tm is the same on every layout.

How do I type ©, ™ and ® on iPhone or iPad?

The iPhone keyboard has no key for any of them. The reliable fix is a text replacement: add one on your Mac and it syncs over, or create it on the iPhone in SettingsGeneralKeyboardText Replacement, with © as the phrase and something like ;copy as the shortcut. The emoji keyboard has ©️, ™️, and ®️ too, but as emoji, which can look out of place in a sentence.

More © ™ ® questions

What’s the HTML code for ©, ™, and ®?

&copy; (or &#169;) for ©, &trade; (&#8482;) for ™, and &reg; (&#174;) for ®. ℠ has no named entity, so use &#8480;. ℗ is &copysr; or &#8471;.

Can I put © on my work without registering it?

In the US, yes. The Copyright Office says using a notice doesn’t require its permission or a registration. Registration is a separate process.

Why does ⌥2 type € instead of ™?

You’re probably on the British layout, where the euro took that key. Use ⇧ ⌥ 2 for ™, or switch layouts in System SettingsKeyboardText InputEdit….

How do I type ©, ™, and ® on Windows?

Hold Alt and type 0169 for ©, 0153 for ™, or 0174 for ® on the numeric keypad, or press Win + . and look under Symbols.
